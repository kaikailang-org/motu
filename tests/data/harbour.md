# Chapter One: The Harbour

<!-- Open on the ferry, not on the town. Keep the weather out of the first line. -->

The ferry came in after dark.[^ferry] Nobody on the quay had expected it, and for a long moment the lamps along the breakwater went on burning as if the boat were only a rumour that the wind had carried in from the open water. Then the engine coughed twice, the hull knocked against the old tyres that hung from the pier, and a boy with a coil of rope over his shoulder ran out from the shelter of the fish market and shouted for someone to take the bow line.

Ana stood at the rail with her bag between her feet. She had been awake since the mainland, since the grey hour when the ticket office opened and the woman behind the glass had looked at her name for a long time before she stamped the slip. Now she watched the town assemble itself out of the dark: the church first, because its tower was lit from below; then the line of houses climbing the hill, each with one window burning; then the harbour itself, which smelled of diesel and kelp and something sweet that she could not name and that she would later learn was the bakery on the corner of *Calle Larga*, where the ovens were lit at nine so that the bread would be ready for the boats at four.

"You're the teacher," the boy said when she stepped down onto the stones. It was not a question.

"I'm the teacher," she said.

He nodded as if that settled something, and took her bag without asking, and walked off up the quay without looking back to see whether she followed.

## The house on the hill

The house they had given her stood at the top of a lane so steep that the steps had been cut into the rock itself. There were forty-one of them. Ana counted them the first night, because counting was something to do with her mind while her legs burned, and she counted them again every night for the rest of that winter, until the number stopped being a number and became simply the shape of going home.

The boy, whose name was Tomás, set her bag down inside the door and showed her the things that mattered:

- where the fuse box was, behind a curtain in the kitchen;
- which tap gave hot water and which only pretended to;
- the window in the bedroom that did not close unless you lifted it first;
- the lamp, an old brass thing with a glass chimney, that he said she would need.

"The lights fail," he said. "Not every night. But when the wind comes from the south, they fail."

"How often does the wind come from the south?"

He thought about it with the seriousness of a child asked a question by an adult who actually wanted the answer. "Mostly in winter," he said at last. "Mostly at night."

When he had gone she sat on the edge of the bed with her coat still on and listened to the house. It was a small house and it made small noises: the tick of the stove cooling, the drip of the tap that pretended to give hot water, a shutter somewhere that moved on its hinge and stopped and moved again. Below her, through the window that would not close, she could hear the sea working at the rocks with the patience of something that had all the time there was.

She thought about the letter in her bag. She did not take it out. She had read it so many times on the ferry that the folds had gone soft as cloth, and she knew what it said, and knowing did not help.

> The school has been without a teacher since March. The children are good children, but they have been allowed to run wild, and some of the parents have stopped believing that they will ever be anything but fishermen. We would be grateful for anyone. We would be grateful for you.
>
> — *from the letter of the mayor, dated the fourth of September*

Anyone, she thought. Grateful for anyone. It was an honest letter, at least. She had received less honest ones.

## The night the lights failed

The wind came from the south on her third night.

She was at the kitchen table marking the exercises she had set that afternoon — a page of sums for the younger ones, a short composition for the older — when the bulb above her head flickered, steadied, flickered again and went out. For a moment the dark was total. Then her eyes found the grey square of the window and the faint red glow of the stove, and she sat very still, the pencil still in her hand, and listened.

The town had gone dark with her. She could tell by the quality of the silence, which was different from the silence of a single house: it was wider, and it had edges, and somewhere inside it a dog was barking with the steady outrage of an animal that has been personally insulted. Down at the harbour a bell began to ring. It was not the church bell. It was smaller and higher, and it rang in a pattern — three, a pause, three, a pause — that she would later learn meant *the lights are out, check your moorings*, and that every child in the town could have explained to her if she had thought to ask.

She found the brass lamp by touch, and the matches in the drawer beside it where Tomás had shown her, and on the second try she got the wick to take. The flame rose yellow and uncertain behind the chimney and the kitchen came back to her in a new shape, smaller and warmer and full of moving shadows.

There was a knock at the door.

It was a woman of about sixty, wrapped in a shawl, with a lantern of her own and a covered basket on her arm. "Señora Ibarra," she said, by way of introduction. "From below. You have oil?"

"I have a lamp," Ana said. "I don't know how much oil is in it."

Señora Ibarra came in without being asked, as everyone in that town came in without being asked, and lifted the lamp and shook it gently and listened to the slosh. "Enough for tonight," she said. "Tomorrow you buy more from Benito. Not from the shop on the square. Benito." She set the basket on the table and drew back the cloth. Inside was half a loaf of bread, a wedge of hard white cheese, and a bottle with no label. "For the dark," she said.

"Thank you. You didn't have to —"

"No," Señora Ibarra agreed. "Nobody has to." She sat down across the table, uninvited, and looked at the exercise books stacked by Ana's elbow. "Those are the children's?"

"Some of them. The ones who came."

"How many came?"

"Eleven."

The older woman made a noise in her throat that might have been a laugh. "There are thirty-four children in this town of an age for school," she said. "Eleven is good, for the first week. Eleven is very good."

## What the children knew

By the end of the month there were nineteen.

They came in ones and twos, sent by mothers who had heard from other mothers that the new teacher did not shout, or by fathers who had been told by their own children at supper that the new teacher knew the names of the stars. This last was not quite true. Ana knew the names of perhaps a dozen stars, and of the constellations only the ones that anyone knows; but on the first clear night after the lights failed she had taken the older class up onto the headland above the lighthouse, and pointed, and told them what she knew, and admitted what she did not.

That admission, she came to understand, had done more for her than anything else she could have said. The children of that town had been told a great many things by a great many adults, and most of it had turned out to be wrong; they had developed, as a result, an exquisite sensitivity to the moment when a grown-up stopped knowing and started pretending. Ana had not pretended. She had said: *I don't know what that one is called. Let's find out.* And the next week a parcel had come on the ferry from the mainland with a book in it, a star atlas with a cracked spine and someone else's name written inside the cover, and she had brought it to school and set it on her desk and left it there, open, for anyone to look at.

The children knew other things, too — things she had no book for.

1. They knew the tides, not as a table of numbers but as a feeling in the body, the way you know whether a room is warm.
2. They knew every boat in the harbour by its sound, and could tell you from the schoolroom window whose engine was coming in and whether it was running rough.
3. They knew which of the old men on the bench by the fish market could be trusted to tell the truth and which would tell you a better story instead.
4. They knew, all of them, without ever having been told, that the lighthouse keeper's daughter had not drowned by accident.

The fourth thing Ana did not learn for a long time. When she did learn it, she learned it from Tomás, who told her on the steps of the school one evening in February while they waited for his mother, and who told it flatly, without drama, the way you would mention that it was going to rain.

"Everybody knows," he said. "Nobody says."

"Why doesn't anybody say?"

He looked at her with something like pity. "Because then you'd have to do something," he said.

## A note on the lamps

It should be said, for the sake of the record, that the lights of the town did not fail by chance.[^power] The line that brought power across the strait from the mainland had been laid in the year the old dictator died, and it had been laid badly, by a contractor who had since become a minister; and every winter the southern gales worked at the place where it came ashore below the cemetery, and every winter the joint there filled with salt water, and the whole town went dark.

Everyone knew this, too. There had been petitions. There had been a visit, once, from an engineer in a white car who had walked down to the cemetery in polished shoes and looked at the place for a long time and written things in a notebook, and had gone away again, and had not come back. After that the town had stopped writing petitions and had started buying oil from Benito, and the bell at the harbour had acquired its pattern of three-and-a-pause, and the children had learned to do their homework by lamplight on the nights when the wind came from the south.

Ana found that she did not mind the dark as much as she had expected. There was something in it that she had not had on the mainland, a kind of permission. In the dark you did not have to be anyone in particular. You could sit at the kitchen table with your hands around a cup of Señora Ibarra's unlabelled brandy and listen to the sea, and nobody would ask you why you had come, or what you had left behind, or whether you meant to stay.

She did not know whether she meant to stay. That was the truth. She had come because the letter had said *anyone*, and she had needed, that autumn, very badly, to be anyone.

## The lighthouse

The lighthouse stood at the end of the headland, a white tower with a red band at the top, and it was the one light in the town that never failed. It had its own generator, an old diesel that thudded in a concrete shed at the foot of the tower, and when the rest of the town went dark the beam went on sweeping across the water as if nothing had happened: four seconds of light, eleven of dark, four of light.

The keeper was a man called Arturo Vidal. He was perhaps fifty, though he looked older, with a beard that had gone white on one side before the other, and he came down into the town only on Thursdays, for supplies. On Thursdays the children went quiet when he passed. Not frightened, exactly. Careful. The way you go quiet when you pass a house where someone has died.

Ana met him for the first time in Benito's shop, buying oil. He was ahead of her at the counter and he turned when the bell over the door rang and looked at her for a long moment, the way the woman at the ticket office had looked at her name.

"The teacher," he said.

"Yes."

"My daughter would have been in your school." He said it without emphasis, as if he were telling her the price of the oil. "She would have been fifteen this year."

Ana did not know what to say. She said, "I'm sorry."

"Yes," said Arturo Vidal. "Everybody is." He paid for his oil and took it and went out, and the bell over the door rang behind him, and Benito, who had been standing very still behind the counter throughout, let out a long breath and began to wrap Ana's bottle in newspaper with hands that were not quite steady.

"You should not talk to him," Benito said.

"I didn't. He talked to me."

"Then you should not answer." He pushed the bottle across the counter and would not meet her eyes. "That is all I will say. I have a family, you understand. That is all."

## Composition

That week she set the older class a composition. The subject was **a place in the town that you love**, and she expected — she would admit this later, to herself, with some shame — that she would get nineteen essays about the harbour, or the beach below the church, or the football pitch behind the cannery that flooded every spring.

She got eighteen of those. The nineteenth was from a girl called Inés, who sat at the back by the window and who had not, in five weeks, said more than a dozen words in class. It was about the lighthouse.

> *I love the lighthouse because it is the only thing in this town that tells the truth. It says: here are the rocks. It says it every fifteen seconds, all night, every night, and it does not care who is listening. When the lights go out in the town, the lighthouse does not go out. Marisol used to say that the lighthouse was the bravest thing on the island. Marisol was my friend. She is not here any more.*

Ana read it three times. Then she put it at the bottom of the pile and marked the others, and when she came back to it at the end it was still there, and still said what it said.

She did not give it a mark. She wrote at the bottom, in pencil, *Thank you for telling me.* And then, after a long time, beneath that: *Would you like to talk about Marisol?*

The next morning the composition was on her desk when she arrived, before anyone else, with a single word written under her question in a small careful hand.

*Yes.*

## What Inés said

They talked in the schoolroom after the others had gone, with the door open, because Ana had been a teacher long enough to know that some conversations need an open door. Inés sat at her own desk by the window and Ana sat at the desk in front of it, turned around, so that they were not quite facing each other; and the girl looked out at the harbour while she talked, and Ana looked at her hands.

What Inés said was this.

Marisol Vidal had been her best friend since they were six. They had done everything together. They had a hiding place in the rocks below the lighthouse, a cleft you could only reach at low tide, where they kept a tin box with their treasures in it — a gull's skull, a glass float from a Japanese net, a photograph of a film star cut out of a magazine. On the night Marisol died the lights had failed, and the two of them had gone down to the hiding place in the dark, because the dark was exciting and because the tide was very low, lower than either of them had ever seen it.

"And there was a boat," Inés said. "In the cove. With no lights."

Ana waited.

"There were men unloading it. Boxes. They didn't see us at first. Then one of them did." The girl's voice had not changed at all; it was as flat and careful as her handwriting. "Marisol ran one way and I ran the other. I got home. She didn't."

"Did you tell anyone?"

"I told my father." Inés turned from the window, at last, and looked at her. "He told me I had been dreaming. He said if I told anyone else I would be dreaming in the cemetery." She paused. "He said it nicely. He was frightened. He is still frightened."

"Do you know who the men were?"

Inés did not answer. She did not need to. She looked out of the window again, down the hill, to where the lights of the cannery were just coming on in the dusk, and to the white car that was parked, as it was parked every evening, in front of the cannery office. It was the same white car, Ana realised, that had brought the engineer to look at the power line below the cemetery. The engineer who had written things in a notebook, and gone away, and not come back.

## Afterwards

She walked home that night by the long way, along the harbour wall and up past the church, because she did not want to go past the cannery. The wind had gone round to the south again during the afternoon and she could feel it building, heavy and warm and full of rain, and she knew that before she reached the top of the forty-one steps the lights would fail.

They failed at the twenty-third step. She stood in the dark with her hand on the cold rock of the wall and waited for her eyes to adjust, and as she waited she heard the bell begin down at the harbour — three, a pause, three, a pause — and then, beneath it, very faint, the thud of an engine that was not the lighthouse generator. A boat. Coming in without lights, into the cove below the headland, while the whole town sat in the dark and checked its moorings and did not look.

*Everybody knows*, Tomás had said. *Nobody says.*

Ana climbed the rest of the steps by feel. At the top she let herself into the house and did not light the lamp. She sat at the kitchen table in the dark with her coat on, the way she had sat on the first night, and she listened to the sea, and she thought about the letter in her bag that said *anyone*, and about what it would mean to stop being anyone and become, instead, someone in particular.

Then she got up, and lit the lamp, and took a clean sheet of paper from the drawer, and began to write.[^letter]
